Pattern

Content and tone

Every word a child sees in TwinkleTune is encouragement. Twinkle praises what went well, offers one thing to try next, and never says "wrong". This page is the voice, the words we use and avoid, and the microcopy library drawn from the mocks.

Overview

TwinkleTune has two readers. Children (about four to ten) hear Twinkle, a star coach who is always on their side. Grown-ups read short, plain, respectful sentences in the Grown-Ups Corner and its tools. The content foundation covers casing, numbers and punctuation; this pattern covers voice and what to say in each moment.

Voice principles

  • Encouragement only. There is no failure state in the words. The lowest result is Brave Singing, Ada! 🎉; a missed note says nothing at all.
  • Specific praise, then one next step. Your notes sparkled! The “My fair lady!” part was tricky — want to practice just that bit? The tricky part is an offer, never a penalty.
  • Few words, big meaning. A five-year-old should get it from the emoji and the first three words.
  • Her voice is the right voice. Songs move to fit her (Every song in YOUR key 💙); she is never asked to fit the song.
  • Warm, not babyish. Playful images (a big cozy “laaa”, squeak up high like a baby bird, like a comfy sweater) without talking down.

Structure

The hardest test of the voice is the lowest result. Results for a one-star song: the headline is praise, the score says what she landed (never what she missed), Twinkle names the tricky part as an offer, and the gold button makes practice feel like a reward.

You landed 12 of 27 notes! 🎯

Sung in YOUR key — high five for those notes!

Your notes sparkled! The “Merrily, merrily, merrily, merrily” part was tricky — want to practice just that bit?
Practice the tricky part 🎯
Sing again 🔁New song 🎵

Pale stars are "still to earn", never crossed out. The bar fills to what she landed; nothing counts what she missed.

Message formula

  1. Lead with what went well or what is happening (Your voice is found! 🎉).
  2. Make it hers: her name, her key, her range (You sing from B3 up to E5.).
  3. Offer one next step as a question or an invitation (want to practice just that bit?).
  4. End warm: one emoji, usually 💙 for comfort, ✨ for sparkle, 🎉 for celebration.

Responsive behaviour

  • Write for a 320px-wide phone first: a button label must fit on one line at 360px, a screen title in one line at 32px, a toast in one line most of the time.
  • Copy is the same at every width. Never write a longer desktop variant; the column is the same 470px everywhere.
  • Twinkle's bubble may wrap to three lines; if it needs four, cut a clause.
  • Leave room for translation: labels that are 20% longer must still fit, so avoid packing a button to its edge in English.

Accessibility

  • Plain words at a young reading age: short sentences, common words, one idea each. Grown-up copy aims for a reading age of about twelve.
  • Emoji support, never replace. Every emoji-led label still has words; icon-only buttons carry an aria-label that says the whole action (Sing London Bridge again). Decorative emoji are aria-hidden="true"; meaningful ones are text and are read as such, so keep one per string.
  • Accessible names match the visible words (WCAG 2.5.3): Keep singing ▶ is named "Keep singing". Page objects in the end-to-end tests rely on these names too.
  • No meaning in colour or position words. Never "tap the gold button" or "the one on the right"; name the action.
  • Timing: text that disappears (toasts, cheers) is never the only place a message lives.

Content

The encouragement test

Every new string a child can see must pass all five before it ships:

  • It says what went well or what to try, not what went wrong.
  • It contains none of the words in the "never" column below.
  • It would feel kind if read aloud to a five-year-old who just tried her hardest.
  • It offers at most one next step, and that step is optional.
  • Silence, a missed note or a quiet microphone gets no comment at all.
